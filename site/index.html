Before you start

No finance background is needed to read this page. These are the seven assets and the four ideas behind every chart.

The assets

  • S&P 500: an index of the 500 largest US companies, often used as shorthand for “the market”. It is drawn in a neutral colour (black, or white in dark mode) and serves as the benchmark the other assets are compared with.
  • Four US companies from different sectors: Apple (AAPL, technology), JPMorgan Chase (JPM, banking), Exxon Mobil (XOM, oil and gas) and Johnson & Johnson (JNJ, healthcare).
  • Two cryptocurrencies: Bitcoin (BTC-USD) and Ether (ETH-USD), priced in US dollars. They trade every day, including weekends.

  • Return

    How much an investment gained or lost, as a percentage. +25% means that 100 dollars became 125. Dividends are included, as if they had been reinvested.

  • Volatility

    How much the price jumps around from one day to the next, scaled to a full year. Higher volatility means a bumpier ride and a wider range of possible outcomes. It says nothing about whether the price went up or down.

  • Drawdown

    The fall from the highest price reached so far to a later low. The maximum drawdown is the worst of these falls: what someone who bought at the top would have lost at the worst moment.

  • Correlation

    Whether two assets tend to rise and fall on the same days. It goes from −1 (always opposite) through 0 (unrelated) to +1 (always together).

Use the buttons below to choose a period and to hide or show assets. Every chart and every summary underneath it updates with your selection.

Performance

Cumulative return since the start of the period, from closing prices adjusted for splits and dividends. Each asset starts at 0%.

How to read it. Each line answers the question “if I had invested at the start of the period, how much would I have gained or lost by this date?”. A line at +50% means 100 dollars had become 150; a line at −20% means it had shrunk to 80. Lines above the neutral S&P 500 line did better than the market. Hover over or tap the chart to read the values on any date.

Risk

Return is only half of the picture: two assets can end the period at the same place after very different journeys. Risk measures how rough that journey was.

Rolling volatility

Calculated over the last 20 or 60 observations.

How to read it. Each point measures how much the price moved during the previous 20 (or 60) trading days, expressed per year. As a rough guide, a volatility of 20% means that a typical year ends within about 20% above or below the average outcome. When a line jumps, the market was going through a stressful period, such as the start of the pandemic in March 2020 (choose “Since 2018” to see it). The 20-day window reacts quickly; the 60-day window is smoother.

Technically, it is the standard deviation of daily returns, multiplied by √252 for equities and the index (trading days per year) and by √365 for crypto, which trades every day.

Maximum drawdown

Largest fall from a previous peak within the period.

How to read it. The longer the bar, the deeper the worst fall. −30% means that, at the worst moment, 100 dollars invested at the peak were worth 70. Losses are harder to undo than they look: after a 50% fall, the price has to double just to get back to where it was. Hover over a bar to see the dates of the peak and the low.

Return and volatility

Annualised over the period.

How to read it. Each dot is one asset. Further right means a bumpier ride; higher up means a better yearly return. Dots towards the top left earned more for each unit of risk taken. An index spread over 500 companies usually sits furthest to the left, because the ups and downs of single companies partly cancel out.

Correlation

Pearson correlation of returns. Each pair uses only the dates on which both assets traded, so a crypto weekend move is compared with the equity return over the same days.

−1+1

How to read it. Find one asset in the rows and another in the columns: the cell where they meet says how closely their daily movements are linked. Blue cells move together, pink cells tend to move in opposite directions, and pale cells are largely unrelated. The diagonal is always 1, because every asset moves exactly with itself.

Why it matters. Combining assets that do not move together makes a portfolio's ups and downs smaller, because a fall in one is often offset by another. This is the idea behind diversification. Correlation measures how often assets move together, not how much they gain.

How it works

Data is downloaded with yfinance, validated and stored in two tables. No metric is stored: each one is a SQL query run on demand.

  • Ingestion

    Incremental daily updates. The last stored days are re-downloaded and compared, so a dividend that changes past adjusted prices triggers a full reload.

  • Storage

    SQLite with strict types, a composite (asset_id, date) key and CHECK constraints that reject inconsistent prices.

  • Analytics

    LAG, FIRST_VALUE and rolling window sums. SQLite has no STDDEV or CORR, so both are written out and tested against pandas.
